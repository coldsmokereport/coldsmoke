Atmospheric rivers, what are they? Is it just another overhyped named weather phenomenon by the media like bomb cyclones, polar vortex, etc? And why am I bringing this up now?

As its name implies, an atmospheric river is essentially a narrow (typically but not always) plume of very moist air, often from tropical origins that becomes a part of a mid-latitude weather system. It often “flows” rapidly and is associated with strong mid-upper level winds that help transport that moisture through the atmosphere. I’m bringing this up, because, the forecast models are showing a decent possibility of an atmospheric river (AR for short) impacting SW Montana this weekend.

The figure below is a graphic from NOAA that explains what an atmospheric river is, better than I can:

![](/assets/images/The_science_behind_atmospheric_rivers.jpg)

To apply the atmospheric river schematic to this actual storm, below is a cross-section of topographic terrain (artificially / AI enhanced, I know boo AI; I promise everything else was written by me or credited). I wanted to share this figure because I think it graphically demonstrates very well how the moisture is highest at the Pacific Northwest Coast (IVT or integrated Water Vapor Transport; higher = more moisture) and gradually depletes after each successive mountain range, the most dramatic drops occurring following the Wallowas and Bitterroot ranges. By the time it reaches Bridger/Big Sky the moisture is now less than 60% of what it was just the day prior along the coastline. One challenge with the models, is that they often don’t properly resolve terrain (see our post that covered a separate forecast degradation caused by this: ), so the amount of moisture depletion may be underestimated and is often incorrect.

![](/assets/images/ivt_terrain3d_v4.png)

Despite this depletion and the model uncertainties, though, it can still produce prolific rain and snowfall amounts in interior regions. For the upcoming AR this weekend, right now there is considerable uncertainty. The European model have been much more consistent in showing significant snowfall and a stronger AR event for SW Montana than the GFS. Notice for the GFS how each previous model run has shown a lot of variability, from some of the highest totals and largest IVT region, to some of the smallest. Thus, if I had to choose I’d probably place a little more confidence in the European models currently.

![](/assets/images/band_runs_precip4_v6b_12z.png)

What does this mean for the forecast? It’s too early to get into specifics, since the main moisture plume is still well offshore. However, tentatively, I’d say 3–7+ inches is a safe bet at Bridger and Big Sky. If I had to put odds down, I’d say the odds are definitely higher of me having to bump up those #s tomorrow/Thursday a bit vs. lowering them. What is almost a guarantee? It will be windy. Almost every model solution is showing 50–75+ MPH gust potential on ridgelines with strong westerly winds. For those skiing at Big Sky, this means you should be prepared for periodic lift stoppages on the upper mountains this weekend. Hopefully as the forecast gets a little closer in, I can pinpoint the timing a little more to help folks plan out their ski days a little more.
