Atmospheric rivers; what are they? Are they just another overhyped, media-named weather phenomenon, like bomb cyclones, the polar vortex, etc.? And why am I bringing this up now?

As its name implies, an atmospheric river is essentially a narrow (typically, but not always) plume of very moist air, often of tropical origin, that becomes part of a mid-latitude weather system. It often “flows” rapidly and is associated with strong mid-to-upper level winds that help transport that moisture through the atmosphere. I’m bringing this up because the forecast models are showing a decent possibility of an atmospheric river (AR for short) impacting SW Montana this weekend.

The figure below is a graphic from NOAA that explains what an atmospheric river is, better than I can:

To apply the atmospheric river schematic to this actual storm, below is a 3-D view of the terrain from the Oregon coast to SW Montana (heights exaggerated so the ranges stand out; made with some AI help, I know, boo AI; I promise everything else was written by me or credited). I wanted to share this figure because I think it shows very well how the moisture is highest at the Pacific Northwest Coast (IVT or integrated Water Vapor Transport; higher = more moisture being carried inland) and gradually depletes after each successive mountain range, with the biggest drops across the Blue Mountains/Wallowas and then the Bitterroots. By the time it reaches Bridger/Big Sky, it's down to about 60% of what it was just the day prior along the coastline. One challenge with the models is that they often don’t properly resolve terrain (see our [post](https://coldsmokereport.com/posts/storm-recap/) that covered a separate forecast degradation caused by this: ), so they can improperly estimate the amount of moisture depletion along the way.

![](/assets/images/ivt_terrain3d_v4.png)

_Terrain from the Oregon coast to SW Montana (vertical scale exaggerated) with the forecast moisture flow, Thursday evening through Saturday morning, averaged from the ECMWF, ECMWF AI, GFS and GEFS models. Imagery: NASA Blue Marble; terrain: NOAA ETOPO1_

Despite this depletion and the model uncertainties, though, ARs can still produce prolific rain and snowfall amounts in interior regions. For the upcoming AR this weekend, right now there is considerable uncertainty. The European models have been much more consistent in showing significant snowfall and a stronger AR event for SW Montana than the GFS. Notice how much the GFS has varied from run to run, from some of the highest totals and largest precipitation areas to some of the smallest. Thus, if I had to choose, I’d probably place a little more confidence in the European models currently.

![](/assets/images/band_runs_precip4_v6b_12z.png)

What does this mean for the forecast? It’s too early to get into specifics, since the main moisture plume is still well offshore. However, tentatively, I’d say 3–7+ inches is a reasonable first call at Bridger and Big Sky. If I had to put odds on it, I'm more likely to bump those numbers up a bit tomorrow/Thursday than lower them. What is almost a guarantee? It will be windy. Almost every model solution is showing 50–75+ MPH gust potential on ridgelines with strong westerly winds, especially Thursday night through Friday. For those skiing at Big Sky, this means you should be prepared for periodic lift stoppages on the upper mountains.
