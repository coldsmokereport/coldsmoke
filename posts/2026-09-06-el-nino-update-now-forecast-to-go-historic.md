Back in July we [ran 76 winters of the record against the Oceanic Niño
Index](%BASE%/posts/el-nino-and-bridger-bowl-what-76-winters-say/) and
concluded that the developing El Niño tilted the odds against a big Bridger
winter — modestly, and mostly by deleting the blower years. That post was
built on the Climate Prediction Center's July numbers: Niño-3.4 at **+1.2 °C**
and an 81% chance of a *very strong* event by fall.

A month later, the forecast has gotten louder. Here's the update, and — the
honest part — why the snow implication only nudged.

## What CPC actually said

The August 13 ENSO discussion (the alert status is now a full **El Niño
Advisory**) reads like a warming ocean picking up speed:

- **Niño-3.4 is at +1.4 °C** for July, with Niño-3 at +1.7 °C and the coastal
  Niño-1+2 region all the way up at **+2.9 °C**. Eastern-Pacific surface
  anomalies are already past +2.0 °C.
- The **subsurface is loaded** — a deep pool of warm water running about
  +10 °C above average is queued up to surface through the fall.
- The atmosphere has coupled: westerly wind anomalies across the equatorial
  Pacific, enhanced convection near the Date Line, and a Southern Oscillation
  Index running near two standard deviations negative.

Two headline numbers jumped from July:

| | July basis | **August 13** |
|---|---|---|
| Niño-3.4 | +1.2 °C | **+1.4 °C** |
| Chance of a *very strong* event | 81% | **> 90%** |
| Chance of a *record* event | — | **69%** |

That last row is the new story. CPC now puts a **69% chance on a historic
event** — one that would exceed the strength of *any* El Niño back to 1950,
defined as a three-month RONI of **+2.5 °C or more** for October–December. If
it verifies, this is 1997-98 / 2015-16 territory or beyond.

## So does that torch our winter?

Less than you'd think — and this is where having run the numbers pays off. We
re-centered the outlook on the stronger forecast (an ONI around +2.4, the top
of the historical analog set) and re-ran the same three framings from July:
the raw strong-plus analogs, an ONI-distance-weighted blend of all 76 winters,
and the quantile-regression fit.

![2026-27 outlook, August refresh](%BASE%/assets/images/2026-09-06/outlook_plume_aug.png)

They still converge, just a hair lower than July:

- **Central expectation ≈ 140–145 inches**, about **85–88% of a normal
  season** (climo mean 164″). July's read was 145–150″; the stronger forecast
  shaved a few inches off, not thirty.
- **10th–90th percentile ≈ 110″ to 165″.**
- **~76% chance of finishing below the climo mean**, ~30% chance of a lean
  year under 130″, and — as in July — **essentially no chance of a 200″
  blower season**. El Niño caps the ceiling; it doesn't drop the floor out.

The reason a "record" forecast barely moves the snow number is the same reason
the July post led with humility: at Bridger, **ENSO explains only about 14% of
what a winter does.** A stronger signal loads the dice a little more firmly,
but the other 86% — the polar jet, the storm-by-storm luck, whatever the North
Pacific decides in February — is untouched by how warm the eastern Pacific
gets.

## The analogs, and where they run out

![Very-strong analog winters at Bridger](%BASE%/assets/images/2026-09-06/analog_table_aug.png)

Our three very-strong analogs (DJF ONI ≥ 2.0) are 1982-83, 1997-98, and
2015-16. They delivered **167″, 112″, and 138″** — a mean near 139″, and a
spread wide enough to contain both a perfectly respectable winter and a
genuinely lean one. That range *is* the forecast.

But note the caveat that comes with a *record* event: **no winter in our
record exceeds about +2.6 in DJF ONI.** If OND RONI really does clear +2.5,
2026-27 would sit at or past the edge of everything we have to compare it to.
The "ceiling-capping" relationship we lean on is measured *inside* the analog
envelope; push beyond it and we're extrapolating. (A technical wrinkle, too:
RONI — which strips out the long-term tropical warming trend — isn't the same
index as the ONI these analogs are binned on. The +2.4 we plugged in is a
translation, not CPC's RONI figure.) Fewer close comparisons means wider
honest error bars, not a lower number.

## Bottom line

The forecast got more dramatic; the playbook didn't. Same as July, only more
so:

- **Plan for a slow start.** The suppression is worst in November–December.
- **Expect fewer deep days**, a central total around 85–90% of normal, and
  real odds (~30%) of a lean year — but don't write off the season. 1982-83
  was a record El Niño and still beat climatology.
- **The snow that does fall should still be cold.** There's no reliable
  temperature signal here — this is a storm-track story, not a warm-winter
  one. When it snows, it should still be cold smoke.

CPC's next discussion lands September 10, and the fall forecasts are where a
developing El Niño usually shows its hand. We'll be watching — and once the
flakes fly, the [Season Tracker](%BASE%/tracker/) will trace this winter
against the gray bands in real time.

Think snow anyway.
