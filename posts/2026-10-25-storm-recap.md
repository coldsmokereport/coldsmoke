**The Good**

Overall, the forecasts for Bridger and Bozeman seemed to verify pretty close to expectations. Three to four inches of snow in the Bridgers verified on the upper end of the 2–4" forecast. In town, we saw that quicker transition to snow which led to some snowfall accumulations on grassy surfaces.

**The Bad**

Unfortunately for Big Sky skiers, the storm didn't turn out quite as good as I had originally anticipated. Snowfall totals were about 3–4" on the mid-upper mountains, which was at the low end or slightly less than the 4–8" forecasted.

First, I'll add that as I had previously put in my first post disclaimer, I will be wrong at times :D. Admitedly, as primarily a Bridger skier, I'm still learning a bit of the nuances with Big Sky snowfall. This is one I probably could have foreseen, but it's all a learning process. So what happened?

First, several of the models seem to have a persistent high bias in precipitation, at least for these early season storms thus far. At least part of the cause behind this is the inability of the models to properly resolve the complex terrain around Big Sky. The highest resolution model we have, the High-Resolution Rapid Refresh (HRRR), is a short term forecast model (forecasts available out to 48 hours). It's resolution is 2 km, wheras the Global models used for medium to long range forecasts typically only have about 9 km resolution or worse. This means that topography in the model shows up as blocks (with peaks often too low) versus the true dynamic, jagged nature of terrain.

![](/assets/images/terrain_shadow_3d_blocky.png "Real mountains vs. model mountains")

Why does this matter for Big Sky and Lone Peak? To the north sit the very prominent Spanish Peaks. These are 11,000 feet and thus when the wind comes from that direction it can lead to enhanced precipitation over that terrain. This, however, though puts Big Sky into a "shadow" effect where there can be localized subsidence and/or precipitation depletion by the upstream Spanish Peaks. This is flow dependent, but in the case of Friday's system this seemed to be one of the biggest major culprits.

![](/assets/images/terrain_transect.png)

The Utah downscaled model ensembles that I sometimes post are an attempt to correct for the coarse model resolution. Often increasing precipitation totals with expected orographic enhancements, however, in the case of Big Sky / Lone Mountain, with northerly flow it can actually result in a rain shadow effect, which the model/downscaling does not correctly capture in this scenario.
