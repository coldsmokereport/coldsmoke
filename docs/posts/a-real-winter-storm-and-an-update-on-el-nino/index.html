A Real Winter Storm? And an Update on El Niño

Tuesday Nov. 17 PM:

Current radar and overnight forecast from the HRRR.

Quick Update Tuesday November 17 AM:

Due to limited time with the real job (out of town at a science meeting this week, so you all can thank me for the snow ;)), I'll keep this short. Overall, the models have converged more over the past few days, which is typical when getting closer in range to an actual storm. Snow should arrive late this afternoon in the mountains, and the cold front will bring the main burst during the evening hours with steady snow continuing through the night. The most recent HRRR model runs agree, and the ECMWF and GEFS ensembles put the middle of the storm around 9–11 PM tonight.

I think the totals from the original post still look solid with an above-average chance of Bridger hitting the upper end (6 inches) or higher, especially above mid-mountain. Big Sky's range looks pretty good to me still. I'll try to provide another short update in the PM as the storm arrives.

The National Weather Service has also issued a Winter Weather Advisory for both the mountains and valleys. Unfortunately, it looks like their website is having issues again, so I'll have to wait to add those totals until things are up again.

Original Post from Saturday (11/14):

We're finally close enough in to the potential storm this next week where I can dive a little bit more into the details. Overall, this looks like our 2nd real winter storm of the season. By "real" I mean a storm that will likely produce accumulating snowfall down to the valley floors and be accompanied by sustained below freezing temperatures (for at least a couple of days).

Overall, the storm timing has been pretty consistent within the models for Tuesday through Wednesday. The overall upper level pattern (which is what drives the storm itself and conditions we experience on the ground) had been pretty consistent, but of course, just as I was starting to make this forecast, there were some additional complicating factors that popped up overnight. There is now disagreement between the ECMWF (European) and GFS (American) models with the ECMWF (and ensembles) showing a weaker storm penetrating inland. This can be seen in the animation below. The ~1/3-1/2 of ensemble members that show a potentially weaker solution have less blue near Gallatin Valley. More blues and a sharper kink in the height pattern (lines) would be an indication of a stronger shortwave trough (where the frontal systems derive their energy from). Hopefully this makes sense but feel free to ask any questions in the comment section. The American model has been holding pretty consistent with a wetter storm and stronger upper level trough pattern.

This divergence gives me a bit of hesitation because generally the European model, overall, tends to be slightly more reliable than the American model. However, for any given storm there are a ton of competing factors, so it's not a one size fits all generalization. Below I wanted to show the trends in the SWE numbers (liquid equivalent of snowfall) from some of the various models.

Snowfall Forecast Numbers: I don't love issuing a forecast with hard numbers when there's this level of uncertainty, but sometimes that's just how it goes. I'm erring slightly on the conservative side here given the ECMWF model trends, however, there's still time for those models to flip back, and that would mean these numbers would likely be too low.

Big Sky above mid mountain: 4–8 Inches

Bridger Bowl above mid mountain: 3–6 Inches

Bozeman: 1–3 Inches

El Niño Update:

The Climate Prediction Center (CPC) put out a short update two days ago. The El Niño is still strengthening (Niño‑3.4 rose from +2.1 to +2.5°C in October), however some of the recent trends and guidance suggest maybe a slightly lower peak than previously anticipated. The chance of a record-breaking event dropped slightly from 70% to about 60% for Nov–Jan.

ECMWF Niño‑3.4 forecast from Oct 1 (red) and observed values (dotted).

Overall, though, the forecast is still quite confident in a very strong El Niño, and one of the strongest ever in recorded history. Do these slight changes have any impact on Bridger's forecast? It's unlikely in my opinion that the small trim in the peak will have any snow implications out here. The original post holds (https://coldsmokereport.com/posts/el-nino-and-bridger-bowl-record-winter/) and it's really going to come down to intraseasonal variability, so do those snow dances and let's hope we get lucky with a couple of patterns that overproduce! Thanks for reading!
