Atmospheric River

Atmospheric rivers; what are they? Are they just another overhyped, media-named weather phenomenon, like bomb cyclones, the polar vortex, etc.? And why am I bringing this up now?

As its name implies, an atmospheric river is essentially a narrow (typically, but not always) plume of very moist air, often of tropical origin, that becomes part of a mid-latitude weather system. It often “flows” rapidly and is associated with strong mid-to-upper level winds that help transport that moisture through the atmosphere. I’m bringing this up because the forecast models are showing a decent possibility of an atmospheric river (AR for short) impacting SW Montana this weekend.

The figure below is a graphic from NOAA that explains what an atmospheric river is better than I can:

Graphic: NOAA

To apply the atmospheric river schematic to this actual storm, below is a 3-D view of the terrain from the Oregon coast to SW Montana (heights exaggerated so the ranges stand out; made with some AI help, I know, boo AI; I promise everything else was written by me or credited). I wanted to share this figure because I think it shows very well how the moisture is highest at the Pacific Northwest Coast (IVT or integrated Water Vapor Transport; higher = more moisture being carried inland) and gradually depletes after each successive mountain range, with the biggest drops across the Blue Mountains/Wallowas and then the Bitterroots. By the time it reaches Bridger/Big Sky, it's down to about 60% of what it was just the day prior along the coastline. One challenge with the models is that they often don’t properly resolve terrain (see our post that covered a separate forecast degradation caused by this), so they can improperly estimate the amount of moisture depletion along the way.

Terrain from the Oregon coast to SW Montana (vertical scale exaggerated) with the forecast moisture flow, Thursday evening through Saturday morning, averaged from the ECMWF, ECMWF AI, GFS and GEFS models. Imagery: NASA Blue Marble; terrain: NOAA ETOPO1

Despite this depletion and the model uncertainties, though, ARs can still produce prolific rain and snowfall amounts in interior regions. For the upcoming AR this weekend, right now there is considerable uncertainty. The European models have been much more consistent in showing significant snowfall and a stronger AR event for SW Montana than the GFS. Notice how much the GFS has varied from run to run, from some of the highest totals and largest precipitation areas to some of the smallest. Thus, if I had to choose, I’d probably place a little more confidence in the European models currently.

Areas with at least 0.4 inch of storm-total liquid precipitation, Thursday 5 AM–Monday 5 AM, from each model's four most recent runs, oldest (light) to newest (dark)

What does this mean for the forecast? It’s too early to get into specifics, since the main moisture plume is still well offshore. However, tentatively, I’d say 3–7+ inches is a reasonable first call at Bridger and Big Sky. If I had to put odds on it, I'm more likely to bump those numbers up a bit tomorrow/Thursday than lower them. Because of the atmospheric river's narrowness, this means any slight deviation north or south could cause a big bust in either direction for snowfall totals. What is almost a guarantee? It will be windy. Almost every model solution is showing 50–65+ MPH gust potential on ridgelines with strong westerly winds, especially Thursday night through Friday. For those skiing at Big Sky, this means you should be prepared for periodic lift stoppages on the upper mountain. Hopefully as the forecast gets a closer in, I can pinpoint the timing to help folks plan out their ski days a little more. Thanks for reading and if I don’t get an update out tomorrow, Happy Thanksgiving!

-Trey

Saturday, November 21, 2026

Enjoy the Snow

Love seeing everyone getting out for some turns at Bridger! Please please be safe out there, especially on any high elevation north facing terrain steeper than 30º. Anything that held snow from before the most recent storm cycles is likely to be a problem with weak layers near the ground. See the snippet from the Avalanche Centers discussion below. Also, anything below about 8,500 feet is likely to have only 12–18 inches of base depth, so keep those tips up.

The Bridgers for the full Thursday-Saturday storm cycle saw 7–11 inches. Big Sky totals were a little lower and generally came in around 6 inches. 

Part of MT Avalanche Discussion from Friday: "Earlier this week, new snow sparked enthusiasm for early-season turns, and a skier was caught in an avalanche early Wednesday morning in the northern Bridger Range (observation). Others in the same area triggered and observed additional avalanches (observations 1 and 2). These slides all occurred in terrain where October and early November snow existed on the ground, and on wind-loaded slopes where fresh drifts were primed to slide. Thankfully, everyone came out unscathed.

We encourage enthusiasm for the upcoming winter. However, early-season avalanches in southwest Montana have seriously injured and buried skiers (2012 Incident Report), caught and injured hunters (2015 Incident Report), and resulted in tragic fatalities (2017 Incident Report). We want you to enjoy what the mountains have to offer all season and beyond.

Select grassy slopes less than 30 degrees steep for a sure bet from an avalanche standpoint..."

Friday, November 20, 2026

Second Wave Moving Through Tonight

So far we’ve seen about 1–2 inches at Bridger and Big Sky, most of that falling in the early morning hours (Friday). Based on the latest guidance (another 3–6 inches), I think we’ll still fall within the original 4–8 inch forecast. This won’t be a huge base builder, but we’ll still take it, and the cold temperatures are no doubt allowing for some decent snowmaking as well!

Satellite and radar from midnight to 5 p.m. Friday, HRRR forecast through 9 a.m. Saturday.
