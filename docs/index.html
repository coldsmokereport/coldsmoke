Wednesday, October 28, 2026

Recap, Season Check-in, and Looking Ahead

Monday PM's storm finally came in as a favorable snowfall producer for Big Sky! Totals generally ranged from 3–6 inches, with the Bridgers on the lower end.

A reader comment (always feel free to comment and ask questions; I'll answer as I'm able) prompted me to dig into the following question: How are we doing for the season so far? Based on the nearby SNOTEL stations, we've seen about 21" at Bridger and 9" at Big Sky. For Big Sky that's right around the median. Bridger is near the 90th percentile, largely thanks to the storm a couple of weeks ago. However, as many are aware, most of that snow melted out about a week ago, so current snow depths are only a few to ~7 inches. That's near median at Bridger and a bit below at Big Sky.

It’s important to note that this early in the season, a single big storm or a dry spell can swing these numbers dramatically, so don't read too much into them.

Looking ahead, the good news is another storm is on tap for Sunday through Monday. Details are still uncertain, but I'll aim to have a forecast out Thursday PM or Friday AM.

Monday, October 26, 2026

Quick Update Monday PM

I’ll keep this post short and sweet. After being stuck in the fog for much of the day in town (Bozeman), it was nice to finally see the sun peek out. Cold air/snow on the ground plus a quick ridge building led to a brief inversion across our area the past 24–48 hours. With Bridger temperatures peaking in the mid-to-upper 40s vs. the 30s in town, that inversion was quite apparent.

A quick-hitting storm will move in later this evening. Looking west, you can already see the clouds from it moving in.

Currently a line of showers and thunderstorms to our west and south is moving quickly towards our area. This should begin affecting Big Sky and then Bozeman/Bridgers by mid-to-late evening. I'd expect graupel and some gusty winds in the mountains with this initial convective activity (the graupel may limit accumulation but typically provides decent water content). Then maybe some lingering snow showers overnight behind the quick frontal passage.

Observed radar Monday evening and HRRR forecast through 5 a.m. Tuesday.

Overall, I’d expect a few inches of snow for the mountains. This time the flow is out of the WSW, so I'm hopeful that Big Sky won't get skunked like Friday. Currently a Winter Weather Advisory is in effect from 9PM tonight to 6AM Tuesday around Big Sky with the NWS calling for 2–5 inches.

Evening update: Looks like Big Sky is going to hit on the upper end or higher!

-Trey

Sunday, October 25, 2026

Friday Storm Recap

The Good

Overall, the forecasts for Bridger and Bozeman seemed to verify pretty close to expectations. Three to four inches of snow in the Bridgers verified on the upper end of the 2–4" forecast. In town, we saw that quicker transition to snow which led to some snowfall accumulations on grassy surfaces.

The Bad

Unfortunately for Big Sky skiers, the storm didn't turn out quite as well as I had originally anticipated. Snowfall totals were about 3–4" on the mid-upper mountains, which was at the low end or slightly less than the 4–8" forecasted. Just a quick fyi that the SNOTEL looks like it underestimated snowfall accumulation, so the tracker is likely low biased currently.

First, I'll add that as I had previously put in my first post disclaimer, I will be wrong at times :D. Admittedly, as primarily a Bridger skier, I'm still learning a bit of the nuances with Big Sky snowfall. This is one I probably could have foreseen, but it's all a learning process. So what happened?

Several of the models seem to have a persistent high bias in precipitation, at least for these early season storms thus far. This problem seemed to be two-fold in this case. On Friday, the post frontal part of the storm (northerly flow) that favors Bridger vs. Big Sky set up hours earlier than the models had it. The other cause behind the high precipitation bias (especially in the medium to long range forecasts) is the inability of the models to properly resolve the complex terrain around Big Sky. The highest resolution model we have, the High-Resolution Rapid Refresh (HRRR), is a short term forecast model (forecasts available out to 48 hours). Its resolution is 3 km, whereas the Global models used for medium to long range forecasts typically only have about 9 km resolution or worse. This means that topography in the model shows up as blocks (with peaks often too low) versus the true dynamic, jagged nature of terrain.

Why does this matter for Big Sky and Lone Peak? To the north sit the very prominent Spanish Peaks. In the HRRR, Lone Peak tops out at 8,900 ft instead of 11,166 ft, and the Spanish Peaks at 9,550 ft instead of 11,015 ft. When the wind comes from the north it can lead to enhanced precipitation over the Spanish Peaks. This, however, puts Big Sky into a "shadow" effect where there can be localized subsidence and/or precipitation depletion by the upstream Spanish Peaks. This is flow dependent, but in the case of Friday's system this seemed to be one of the biggest culprits. If the models can't properly resolve the terrain, though, they can't fully capture that shadow effect.

North-to-south slice over the Spanish Peaks into Big Sky and up Lone Peak. Tan = real terrain, blue = HRRR's terrain, arrow = north wind.

The Utah downscaled model ensembles that I sometimes post are an attempt to correct for the coarse model resolution. Often increasing precipitation totals with expected orographic enhancements, however, in the case of Big Sky / Lone Mountain, northerly flow actually results in a rain shadow effect, which the downscaling does not capture.

Looking Ahead

I haven't had a chance to look at this next week's forecast much yet, however, it does look like a potential weak storm could bring a couple inches of mountain snow Monday PM through Tuesday. I'll try to have a more detailed forecast tomorrow if it looks like anything of note.
