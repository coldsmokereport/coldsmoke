Sunday, October 25, 2026

Friday Storm Recap

The Good

Overall, the forecasts for Bridger and Bozeman seemed to verify pretty close to expectations. Three to four inches of snow in the Bridgers verified on the upper end of the 2–4" forecast. In town, we saw that quicker transition to snow which led to some snowfall accumulations on grassy surfaces.

The Bad

Unfortunately for Big Sky skiers, the storm didn't turn out quite as well as I had originally anticipated. Snowfall totals were about 3–4" on the mid-upper mountains, which was at the low end or slightly less than the 4–8" forecasted. Just a quick fyi that the SNOTEL looks like it underestimated snowfall accumulation, so the tracker is likely low biased currently.

First, I'll add that as I had previously put in my first post disclaimer, I will be wrong at times :D. Admittedly, as primarily a Bridger skier, I'm still learning a bit of the nuances with Big Sky snowfall. This is one I probably could have foreseen, but it's all a learning process. So what happened?

Several of the models seem to have a persistent high bias in precipitation, at least for these early season storms thus far. This problem seemed to be two-fold in this case. On Friday, the post frontal part of the storm (northerly flow) that favors Bridger vs. Big Sky set up hours earlier than the models had it. The other cause behind the high precipitation bias (especially in the medium to long range forecasts) is the inability of the models to properly resolve the complex terrain around Big Sky. The highest resolution model we have, the High-Resolution Rapid Refresh (HRRR), is a short term forecast model (forecasts available out to 48 hours). Its resolution is 3 km, whereas the Global models used for medium to long range forecasts typically only have about 9 km resolution or worse. This means that topography in the model shows up as blocks (with peaks often too low) versus the true dynamic, jagged nature of terrain.

Why does this matter for Big Sky and Lone Peak? To the north sit the very prominent Spanish Peaks. In the HRRR, Lone Peak tops out at 8,900 ft instead of 11,166 ft, and the Spanish Peaks at 9,550 ft instead of 11,015 ft. When the wind comes from the north it can lead to enhanced precipitation over the Spanish Peaks. This, however, puts Big Sky into a "shadow" effect where there can be localized subsidence and/or precipitation depletion by the upstream Spanish Peaks. This is flow dependent, but in the case of Friday's system this seemed to be one of the biggest culprits. If the models can't properly resolve the terrain, though, they can't fully capture that shadow effect.

North-to-south slice over the Spanish Peaks into Big Sky and up Lone Peak. Tan = real terrain, blue = HRRR's terrain, arrow = north wind.

The Utah downscaled model ensembles that I sometimes post are an attempt to correct for the coarse model resolution. Often increasing precipitation totals with expected orographic enhancements, however, in the case of Big Sky / Lone Mountain, northerly flow actually results in a rain shadow effect, which the downscaling does not capture.

Looking Ahead

I haven't had a chance to look at this next week's forecast much yet, however, it does look like a potential weak storm could bring a couple inches of mountain snow Monday PM through Tuesday. I'll try to have a more detailed forecast tomorrow if it looks like anything of note.

Friday, October 23, 2026

Friday Storm Update: Trending Colder

Friday AM update: The forecast models trended a little colder overnight. Rain this morning will turn to snow in the mountains by this afternoon and in town by late afternoon or early evening. With the colder air arriving a bit earlier, totals should land toward the upper end of (or slightly above) the 2–4"+ for Bridger and 4–8"+ for Big Sky from the forecast earlier this week.

Radar this morning with airport temperatures and winds; the cold front (blue line) is moving in from the west

In Bozeman, it'll be mostly rain during the day, but by late afternoon – early evening, colder air filtering in plus heavy precipitation rates should lead to a snow changeover. Slick spots are definitely possible for the evening commute, and things could get a little dicey around Bozeman Pass and up the canyon toward Big Sky. FYI, the NWS has a Winter Weather Advisory for the Gallatin and Madison mountains from noon to midnight.

Tuesday, October 20, 2026

An October Surprise? Rain or Snow?

Since the storm potential I mentioned late last week is now closer on the horizon, I'll provide a few more details with a rundown of the latest model guidance. The storm system is already taking shape off the Pacific coast and will first impact the Southwest U.S. beginning tomorrow into Thursday. There's actually a decent shot of a somewhat rare severe thunderstorm threat across parts of Southern California, Arizona, and Southern Utah with the SPC issuing a Day 2 enhanced risk outlook centered over Arizona: https://www.spc.noaa.gov/. This is the kind of southern-track storm that El Niño tends to favor. You can go back and see our first post ever that talked a bit about the southern storm track: https://coldsmokereport.com/posts/el-nino-and-bridger-bowl-record-winter/.

Below is today's latest GFS model forecast for Thursday into Saturday. The upper-level low and trough across the Pacific coast and Southwest will move northeast towards our region, bringing with it an increased chance of rain beginning Thursday night.

Also, just FYI, the rain/snow colors above are from the GFS's coarse 0.25° grid. The model's coarse resolution means it resolves the mountains only as a block averaged across 25 km, which mutes the actual prominence and elevation of the narrow Bridger range. Consequently, the cells around Bridger are actually closer to valley elevation in the model. The forecast is still mountain snow in the late afternoon - evening.

The heaviest precipitation will move across the region Friday during the day and into Friday evening. This is one of the rare situations where I'm actually rooting for rain at higher elevations to start. I haven't had a chance to go up and look myself, but I've been concerned about snowfall from the other week faceting on high-elevation, north-facing aspects that get very little sun this time of year. It may get warm enough the next couple of days regardless, but I'd rather be safe than sorry. By Friday PM, rain should change over to snow, heavy at times for the higher elevations. This storm might favor Big Sky a little bit more than the Bridgers due to high initial snow levels.

What are we looking at for totals? Both the GFS and European model ensembles are showing 0.5–1.5" of liquid water equivalent. A lot will come down to how quickly the colder air can filter in but right now I think 4–8+ inches of snow at the upper mountain of Big Sky is a decent possibility.

For Bridger, due to the storm being a bit on the warmer side initially, I'd expect totals to likely be a bit lower, more towards 2–4"+ with higher amounts possible near the ridge.

What about town? Expect mostly rain in Bozeman Friday, around 0.5–1". The cold air arrives as the moisture is moving out, so snow levels likely won't reach the valley floor until things are close to winding down late Friday night. If the moisture hangs around longer, a slushy coating Saturday morning is possible, but I'm not expecting much. (Yes, I remember last time 🙃. I'll be watching the high-resolution models for any sign of the cold air arriving early.)

I'll keep you all updated as the storm gets within range of the higher-resolution short range models. Thanks for checking in!

-Trey
